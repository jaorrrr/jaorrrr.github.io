2026 Senior Division: Round 1

The Online Knowledge Challenge from the 2026 edition, for students aged 15–18. 90 minutes, 60 marks.

Before you start

  • 90 minutes
  • 60 marks
  • Part A: 10 multiple-choice questions (20 marks)
  • Part B: 5 structured problems (40 marks)

Set a timer and work as a team. You may use a scientific calculator and the data sheet. For Part A, choose an answer and check it. For Part B, write your working on paper, then compare it with the worked solution and mark scheme.

Data sheet

  • Acceleration due to gravity: g = 9.81 m s−2
  • Specific heat capacity of water: c = 4.18 kJ kg−1 K−1; density of water 1.00 kg L−1
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ; 0 °C = 273.15 K; 1 year = 8760 h
  • Planck constant h = 6.63 × 10−34 J s; speed of light c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10−19 J
  • Stefan–Boltzmann constant σ = 5.67 × 10−8 W m−2 K−4
  • Molar masses (g mol−1): H 1.008, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00
  • Radiative forcing of CO2: ΔF = 5.35 ln(C/C0) W m−2

Part A: Multiple choice 20 marks

Each question is worth 2 marks. Choose one answer.

Question A1 2 marks

A rooftop solar module has an area of 1.6 m2 and an efficiency of 20%. On a day equivalent to 4.5 peak sun hours at 1000 W m−2, approximately how much electrical energy does it produce?

Answer options for question A1

Worked solution for question A1

Answer: B. Power at peak irradiance: P = 1.6 m2 × 0.20 × 1000 W m−2 = 320 W. Energy: 320 W × 4.5 h = 1440 Wh = 1.44 kWh. Option A forgets the 4.5 hours.

Question A2 2 marks

Over 20 years, the global warming potential (GWP) of methane is about 81, but over 100 years it is only about 28. What is the main reason for this difference?

Answer options for question A2

Worked solution for question A2

Answer: B. GWP compares the warming caused by a pulse of gas with the same mass of CO2 over a chosen time horizon. Methane is removed (mainly by OH radicals) with a lifetime of about 12 years. Averaged over 100 years, its short, intense effect is diluted, while long-lived CO2 keeps warming. Option C is false: the CO2 produced has a GWP of 1 by definition.

Question A3 2 marks

According to Betz's law, what is the maximum fraction of the kinetic energy flux of the wind that an ideal turbine can extract?

Answer options for question A3

Worked solution for question A3

Answer: C. Extracting all the energy would stop the air behind the rotor, so no more air could flow through. Momentum theory gives an optimum when the wind is slowed to one third of its upstream speed, giving a maximum power coefficient of 16/27 ≈ 59.3%.

Question A4 2 marks

The power available to a wind turbine is P = ½ρAv3. If the mean wind speed at a site is 9.0 m s−1 instead of 6.0 m s−1, by what factor does the available power increase?

Answer options for question A4

Worked solution for question A4

Answer: C. P ∝ v3, so the factor is (9.0/6.0)3 = 1.53 = 3.375. This is why turbines are placed on hilltops and offshore.

Question A5 2 marks

The average pH of the surface ocean has fallen from about 8.2 to 8.1 since pre-industrial times. By approximately what percentage has the hydrogen-ion concentration increased?

Answer options for question A5

Worked solution for question A5

Answer: C. pH = −log10[H+], so [H+]new/[H+]old = 108.2 − 8.1 = 100.1 ≈ 1.26, an increase of about 26%.

Question A6 2 marks

Producers in a grassland fix 50 000 kJ m−2 yr−1. Assuming 10% of the energy is transferred between successive trophic levels, how much energy is available to tertiary consumers?

Answer options for question A6

Worked solution for question A6

Answer: C. Producers 50 000 → primary consumers 5000 → secondary consumers 500 → tertiary consumers 50 kJ m−2 yr−1. Tertiary consumers are three transfers away from the producers.

Question A7 2 marks

In a eutrophic lake, which process is most directly responsible for the sharp fall in dissolved oxygen after an algal bloom?

Answer options for question A7

Worked solution for question A7

Answer: B. When the bloom dies, the huge mass of organic matter is broken down by aerobic bacteria, whose respiration consumes dissolved oxygen faster than it can be replaced. During the day algae photosynthesise and release oxygen, and cooler water holds more oxygen, so A and D are wrong.

Question A8 2 marks

Which of the following is a negative (stabilising) feedback in the climate system?

Answer options for question A8

Worked solution for question A8

Answer: D. By the Stefan–Boltzmann law, a warmer surface emits more radiation (∝ T4), which opposes further warming. This is the Planck response. The other three amplify an initial warming, so they are positive feedbacks.

Question A9 2 marks

A geothermal power plant takes heat from a reservoir at 180 °C and rejects heat to cooling water at 30 °C. What is its maximum theoretical (Carnot) efficiency?

Answer options for question A9

Worked solution for question A9

Answer: B. Temperatures must be in kelvin: η = 1 − TC/TH = 1 − 303.15/453.15 = 0.331 ≈ 33%. Options A and D come from wrongly using degrees Celsius.

Question A10 2 marks

A heat pump with a coefficient of performance (COP) of 3.5 delivers 14 kW of heat to a building. At what rate does it extract heat from the outside air?

Answer options for question A10

Worked solution for question A10

Answer: C. Electrical input = 14 kW / 3.5 = 4.0 kW. By conservation of energy, heat delivered = heat extracted + work input, so heat extracted = 14 − 4.0 = 10 kW.

Part B: Structured problems 40 marks

Show all your working. Method marks are awarded even if the final answer is wrong.

Question B1: Decarbonising school heating 10 marks

A school needs 108 000 kWh of heat per year. The heat is supplied by a gas boiler with an efficiency of 90%. Burning natural gas releases 0.20 kg CO2 per kWh of gas burned. The school is considering an air-source heat pump with a seasonal COP of 3.0. Grid electricity has an emission factor of 0.25 kg CO2 per kWh.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Calculate the annual CO2 emissions from the gas boiler, in tonnes. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B1 part a

    Gas burned = 108 000 / 0.90 = 120 000 kWh. Emissions = 120 000 × 0.20 = 24 000 kg = 24 t.

    Marking: 1 mark for dividing by the efficiency; 1 mark for the answer.

  2. (b)

    Part b: Calculate the annual CO2 emissions if the heat pump supplies all the heat. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B1 part b

    Electricity = 108 000 / 3.0 = 36 000 kWh. Emissions = 36 000 × 0.25 = 9000 kg = 9.0 t.

    Marking: 1 mark for the electricity used; 1 mark for the answer.

  3. (c)

    Part c: Calculate the percentage reduction in emissions. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B1 part c

    (24 − 9.0) / 24 × 100 = 62.5%.

    Marking: Allow error carried forward from (a) and (b).

  4. (d)

    Part d: Above what grid emission factor would the heat pump cause more CO2 than the boiler? 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B1 part d

    Break-even when 36 000 × f = 24 000, so f = 0.67 kg CO2 per kWh. This is roughly the factor of a grid that runs mostly on coal.

    Marking: 1 mark for the equation; 1 mark for the answer.

  5. (e)

    Part e: Suggest two reasons why the real reduction might differ from your answer to (c). 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B1 part e

    Any two of: the COP drops in very cold weather, exactly when heat demand is highest; the grid's emission factor varies by hour and season; leaking refrigerant is a powerful greenhouse gas; a poorly insulated building may need higher flow temperatures, lowering the COP; extra demand may be met by fossil-fuel plants (marginal emissions).

    Marking: 1 mark per valid, explained reason.

Question B2: Fuels and combustion 8 marks

Model petrol as pure octane, C8H18 (density 0.74 kg L−1, energy released 44.4 MJ kg−1). Ethanol, C2H5OH, releases 26.8 MJ kg−1.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Write a balanced equation for the complete combustion of octane. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B2 part a

    2C8H18 + 25O2 → 16CO2 + 18H2O (or C8H18 + 12.5O2 → 8CO2 + 9H2O).

    Marking: 1 mark for correct products; 1 mark for balancing.

  2. (b)

    Part b: Calculate the mass of CO2 produced when 1.00 kg of octane burns completely. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B2 part b

    M(C8H18) = 8 × 12.01 + 18 × 1.008 = 114.22 g mol−1, so 1000 g is 8.755 mol. This gives 8 × 8.755 = 70.04 mol CO2, and 70.04 × 44.01 = 3082 g ≈ 3.08 kg.

    Marking: 1 mark for the moles of octane; 1 mark for the mass of CO2.

  3. (c)

    Part c: A car uses 6.0 L of petrol per 100 km. Calculate its CO2 emissions in g km−1. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B2 part c

    Fuel per km = 0.060 L × 0.74 kg L−1 = 0.0444 kg. CO2 = 0.0444 × 3.08 = 0.137 kg ≈ 137 g km−1.

    Marking: Allow error carried forward from (b).

  4. (d)

    Part d: Calculate the mass of CO2 released per MJ of energy for octane and for ethanol. Then explain why bioethanol can still reduce net emissions. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B2 part d

    Ethanol: M = 46.07 g mol−1. 1 kg is 21.71 mol, giving 43.41 mol CO2 = 1.91 kg. Per MJ: octane 3.08/44.4 = 69 g MJ−1; ethanol 1.91/26.8 = 71 g MJ−1. Ethanol releases slightly more CO2 per unit of energy. However, its carbon was absorbed from the air by the crop only months earlier (biogenic carbon), so it is largely balanced by photosynthesis. Net savings depend on fertiliser, farm energy and land-use change.

    Marking: 1 mark for both values; 1 mark for the biogenic-carbon explanation.

Question B3: Estimating and modelling a population 8 marks

To estimate a population of ground beetles, students capture 60 beetles, mark them harmlessly and release them. A week later they capture 75 beetles, of which 15 are marked.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Use the Lincoln–Petersen index to estimate the population size. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B3 part a

    N = MC/R = 60 × 75 / 15 = 300 beetles.

    Marking: 1 mark for the method; 1 mark for the answer.

  2. (b)

    Part b: State two assumptions of this method. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B3 part b

    Any two of: the population is closed (no births, deaths or migration between samples); marks are not lost and do not affect behaviour or survival; marked individuals mix randomly with the population; every individual is equally likely to be caught.

    Marking: 1 mark each.

  3. (c)

    Part c: Chapman's less-biased estimator is N = (M + 1)(C + 1)/(R + 1) − 1. Calculate this estimate. 1 mark

    Worked solution for question B3 part c

    N = 61 × 76 / 16 − 1 = 289.75 − 1 = 288.75 ≈ 289 beetles.

    Marking: 1 mark.

  4. (d)

    Part d: The population grows logistically: dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K), with r = 0.40 yr−1 and K = 500. Calculate the growth rate when N = 300. Then find the population size at which the growth rate is greatest, and that maximum rate. 3 marks

    Worked solution for question B3 part d

    At N = 300: 0.40 × 300 × (1 − 300/500) = 48 beetles per year. The rate is a quadratic in N with its maximum at N = K/2 = 250, where dN/dt = 0.40 × 250 × 0.5 = 50 beetles per year.

    Marking: 1 mark for 48; 1 mark for K/2 = 250; 1 mark for 50.

Question B4: Rainwater harvesting 7 marks

A school has a roof area of 450 m2, and the annual rainfall is 750 mm. On average, 85% of the rain landing on the roof can be collected. The 600 students each flush a toilet twice per school day, using 6.0 L per flush, on 190 school days a year.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Calculate the volume of rainwater collected per year, in m3. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B4 part a

    450 m2 × 0.750 m × 0.85 = 287 m3 (286.9 m3).

    Marking: 1 mark for converting mm to m; 1 mark for the answer.

  2. (b)

    Part b: What percentage of the school's toilet-flushing water could rainwater supply? 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B4 part b

    Demand = 600 × 2 × 6.0 L × 190 = 1 368 000 L = 1368 m3. Fraction = 286.9 / 1368 = 21%.

    Marking: 1 mark for the demand; 1 mark for the percentage.

  3. (c)

    Part c: All the harvested water is pumped up 12 m to a header tank by a pump that is 50% efficient. Calculate the electrical energy used per year, in kWh. 3 marks

    Worked solution for question B4 part c

    Mass = 286 875 kg. Ep = mgh = 286 875 × 9.81 × 12 = 3.38 × 107 J. Electrical energy = 3.38 × 107 / 0.50 = 6.75 × 107 J = 18.8 kWh, which is very small compared with the water saved.

    Marking: 1 mark for mgh; 1 mark for the efficiency; 1 mark for the kWh conversion.

Question B5: Carbon dioxide and global temperature 7 marks

The pre-industrial CO2 concentration was C0 = 280 ppm. Assume the equilibrium temperature change is ΔT = λΔF, with λ = 0.80 K per W m−2. Ignore all other gases and aerosols.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Calculate the radiative forcing and the equilibrium warming for a concentration of 420 ppm. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B5 part a

    ΔF = 5.35 ln(420/280) = 5.35 × 0.405 = 2.17 W m−2. ΔT = 0.80 × 2.17 = 1.7 K.

    Marking: 1 mark each.

  2. (b)

    Part b: Calculate the equilibrium warming for a doubling of CO2 (the climate sensitivity in this model). 1 mark

    Worked solution for question B5 part b

    ΔF = 5.35 ln 2 = 3.71 W m−2, so ΔT = 3.0 K.

    Marking: 1 mark.

  3. (c)

    Part c: At what CO2 concentration would the equilibrium warming reach 1.5 K? 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B5 part c

    ΔF = 1.5 / 0.80 = 1.875 W m−2, so C = 280 × e1.875/5.35 = 280 × 1.42 = 398 ppm, a level the atmosphere passed around 2014.

    Marking: 1 mark for ΔF; 1 mark for C.

  4. (d)

    Part d: Observed warming is about 1.3 K, even though CO2 is above your answer to (c). Give two reasons. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B5 part d

    The climate is not yet at equilibrium: the oceans' large heat capacity delays warming by decades. Aerosol pollution reflects sunlight and masks part of the greenhouse warming. (Other greenhouse gases add warming, so they do not explain the gap.)

    Marking: 1 mark per valid reason.
