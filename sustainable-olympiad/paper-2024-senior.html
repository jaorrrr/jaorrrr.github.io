2024 Senior Division: Round 1

The Online Knowledge Challenge from the 2024 edition, for students aged 15–18. 90 minutes, 60 marks.

Before you start

  • 90 minutes
  • 60 marks
  • Part A: 10 multiple-choice questions (20 marks)
  • Part B: 5 structured problems (40 marks)

Set a timer and work as a team. You may use a scientific calculator and the data sheet. For Part A, choose an answer and check it. For Part B, write your working on paper, then compare it with the worked solution and mark scheme.

Data sheet

  • Acceleration due to gravity: g = 9.81 m s−2
  • Specific heat capacity of water: c = 4.18 kJ kg−1 K−1; density of water 1.00 kg L−1
  • 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ; 0 °C = 273.15 K; 1 year = 8760 h
  • Planck constant h = 6.63 × 10−34 J s; speed of light c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10−19 J
  • Stefan–Boltzmann constant σ = 5.67 × 10−8 W m−2 K−4
  • Molar masses (g mol−1): H 1.008, C 12.01, N 14.01, O 16.00
  • Solar constant S = 1361 W m−2; GWP100 of N2O = 273

Part A: Multiple choice 20 marks

Each question is worth 2 marks. Choose one answer.

Question A1 2 marks

A home battery stores 13.5 kWh and has a round-trip efficiency of 90%. How much energy must be supplied while charging so that it can deliver 13.5 kWh?

Answer options for question A1

Worked solution for question A1

Answer: C. Energy in × 0.90 = 13.5 kWh, so energy in = 13.5 / 0.90 = 15.0 kWh. Option A wrongly multiplies by 0.90.

Question A2 2 marks

The band gap of crystalline silicon is 1.12 eV. What is the longest wavelength of light that can create an electron–hole pair in silicon?

Answer options for question A2

Worked solution for question A2

Answer: C. λ = hc/E = (6.63 × 10−34 × 3.00 × 108) / (1.12 × 1.60 × 10−19) = 1.11 × 10−6 m = 1110 nm, in the near infrared. Longer wavelengths pass straight through.

Question A3 2 marks

What is the approximate maximum efficiency of a single-junction solar cell under ordinary sunlight (the Shockley–Queisser limit)?

Answer options for question A3

Worked solution for question A3

Answer: B. Photons below the band gap are not absorbed, and the excess energy of high-energy photons is lost as heat. Together these limit a single junction to about 33%. 86% is the limit for an infinite stack of junctions under concentrated light.

Question A4 2 marks

Which organisms fix atmospheric nitrogen in the root nodules of legumes such as beans and clover?

Answer options for question A4

Worked solution for question A4

Answer: C. Rhizobium lives symbiotically in root nodules and converts N2 into ammonium using the enzyme nitrogenase. Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrate, and denitrifiers return nitrogen to the air.

Question A5 2 marks

A river sample has a high biochemical oxygen demand (BOD). This most likely indicates:

Answer options for question A5

Worked solution for question A5

Answer: B. BOD measures the oxygen that microorganisms consume while decomposing organic matter (usually over 5 days at 20 °C). A high BOD means a lot of organic pollution, which will lower dissolved oxygen.

Question A6 2 marks

A pumped-storage reservoir holds 1.0 × 106 m3 of water at an average height of 300 m above the turbines. How much gravitational potential energy is stored?

Answer options for question A6

Worked solution for question A6

Answer: C. m = 1.0 × 109 kg. E = mgh = 1.0 × 109 × 9.81 × 300 = 2.94 × 1012 J. Dividing by 3.6 × 109 J per MWh gives 818 MWh.

Question A7 2 marks

Global demand for a resource grows at 7% per year. Approximately how long does it take for demand to double?

Answer options for question A7

Worked solution for question A7

Answer: B. Doubling time = ln 2 / ln 1.07 = 10.2 years. This matches the 'rule of 70': 70/7 = 10 years.

Question A8 2 marks

Taking Earth's albedo as 0.30 and ignoring the atmosphere, what is Earth's effective radiating temperature?

Answer options for question A8

Worked solution for question A8

Answer: A. Absorbed = emitted: S(1 − α)/4 = σT4, so T = (1361 × 0.70 / (4 × 5.67 × 10−8))1/4 = 255 K ≈ −18 °C. The actual average of +15 °C is 33 K warmer because of the natural greenhouse effect.

Question A9 2 marks

How much energy is needed to heat 150 L of water from 15 °C to 55 °C?

Answer options for question A9

Worked solution for question A9

Answer: B. Q = mcΔT = 150 × 4.18 × 40 = 25 080 kJ. Dividing by 3600 kJ per kWh gives 6.97 kWh. Option C is the answer in MJ, not kWh.

Question A10 2 marks

What is meant by 'green hydrogen'?

Answer options for question A10

Worked solution for question A10

Answer: B. Green hydrogen comes from electrolysis powered by renewables. A is 'blue', C is 'brown/black' and D is 'grey' hydrogen, which is how most hydrogen is made today.

Part B: Structured problems 40 marks

Show all your working. Method marks are awarded even if the final answer is wrong.

Question B1: Reusable or single-use? 9 marks

Manufacturing a reusable glass bottle causes 0.40 kg CO2e. Each use cycle (collection, washing and refilling) adds 0.03 kg CO2e. A single-use aluminium can causes 0.17 kg CO2e per use, including recycling.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Find the minimum number of uses for the bottle's footprint per use to be lower than the can's. 3 marks

    Worked solution for question B1 part a

    Need (0.40 + 0.03n)/n < 0.17, so 0.40 < 0.14n and n > 2.86. The bottle must be used at least 3 times.

    Marking: 1 mark for the inequality; 1 mark for solving it; 1 mark for rounding up to a whole number.

  2. (b)

    Part b: Calculate the footprint per use if a bottle is used 20 times, and the percentage saving compared with the can. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B1 part b

    (0.40 + 20 × 0.03)/20 = 1.00/20 = 0.050 kg per use. Saving = (0.17 − 0.050)/0.17 = 71%.

    Marking: 1 mark each.

  3. (c)

    Part c: In reality, after each use a bottle is returned with probability p = 0.90, otherwise it is lost. Show that the expected number of uses is 1/(1 − p), and calculate the expected footprint per use. 4 marks

    Worked solution for question B1 part c

    A bottle is used exactly k times with probability pk−1(1 − p). So E[k] = Σ k pk−1(1 − p) = (1 − p)/(1 − p)2 = 1/(1 − p) = 10 uses. Footprint per use = (0.40 + 10 × 0.03)/10 = 0.070 kg CO2e, still well below the can.

    Marking: 1 mark for the geometric distribution; 1 mark for summing the series (or an equivalent argument); 1 mark for 10 uses; 1 mark for 0.070 kg.

Question B2: A solar farm 8 marks

A solar farm has a peak capacity of 50 MW and a capacity factor of 18%. An average household uses 3500 kWh of electricity per year. Electricity from coal emits 0.95 kg CO2 per kWh.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Calculate the farm's annual electricity output, in GWh. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B2 part a

    50 MW × 8760 h × 0.18 = 78 840 MWh = 78.8 GWh.

    Marking: 1 mark for the method; 1 mark for the answer.

  2. (b)

    Part b: How many average households could this supply? 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B2 part b

    78 840 000 kWh / 3500 kWh = about 22 500 households.

    Marking: Allow error carried forward.

  3. (c)

    Part c: How much CO2 per year is avoided if this output replaces coal-fired electricity? 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B2 part c

    78 840 000 × 0.95 = 7.49 × 107 kg ≈ 74 900 t of CO2.

    Marking: Allow error carried forward.

  4. (d)

    Part d: Give two reasons why the capacity factor of a solar farm is far below 100%. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B2 part d

    Any two of: no output at night; clouds; low sun angle in winter and in the early morning and evening; panels lose efficiency when hot; dust and soiling; inverter and cable losses.

    Marking: 1 mark each.

Question B3: Sewage effluent in a river 8 marks

Upstream of a treatment plant, a river flows at 4.0 m3 s−1 with a BOD of 2.0 mg L−1 and dissolved oxygen (DO) of 9.0 mg L−1. The plant discharges 0.50 m3 s−1 of effluent with a BOD of 60 mg L−1 and DO of 2.0 mg L−1. Assume complete mixing.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Calculate the BOD of the river just below the outlet. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B3 part a

    BOD = (4.0 × 2.0 + 0.50 × 60)/(4.0 + 0.50) = 38/4.5 = 8.4 mg L−1.

    Marking: 1 mark for the flow-weighted mean; 1 mark for the answer.

  2. (b)

    Part b: Calculate the dissolved oxygen just below the outlet. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B3 part b

    DO = (4.0 × 9.0 + 0.50 × 2.0)/4.5 = 37/4.5 = 8.2 mg L−1.

    Marking: 1 mark for the method; 1 mark for the answer.

  3. (c)

    Part c: Ignoring re-aeration, would the DO stay above 5.0 mg L−1 (needed by most fish) once all the BOD has been exerted? Explain. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B3 part c

    The oxygen demand (8.4) exceeds the oxygen available (8.2), so DO would fall to zero: the river would become anoxic and fish would die.

    Marking: 1 mark for comparing the values; 1 mark for the conclusion.

  4. (d)

    Part d: What is the maximum effluent BOD that would keep the DO at or above 5.0 mg L−1? 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B3 part d

    Allowed mixed BOD = 37/4.5 − 5.0 = 14.5/4.5 mg L−1. So (8.0 + 0.50x)/4.5 ≤ 14.5/4.5, giving 0.50x ≤ 6.5 and x ≤ 13 mg L−1.

    Marking: 1 mark for the inequality; 1 mark for the answer.

Question B4: Fertilisers and nitrous oxide 8 marks

Ammonium nitrate (NH4NO3) and urea (CO(NH2)2) are common nitrogen fertilisers.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in each fertiliser. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B4 part a

    NH4NO3: 28.02/80.05 = 35.0%. Urea: 28.02/60.06 = 46.7%.

    Marking: 1 mark each.

  2. (b)

    Part b: A farmer applies 120 kg of nitrogen per hectare to 15 ha using urea. What mass of urea is needed? 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B4 part b

    N needed = 120 × 15 = 1800 kg. Urea = 1800/0.4665 = 3858 kg ≈ 3.86 t.

    Marking: 1 mark for total N; 1 mark for the answer.

  3. (c)

    Part c: 1.0% of the applied nitrogen is emitted as nitrogen in N2O. Calculate the mass of N2O and its CO2-equivalent. 3 marks

    Worked solution for question B4 part c

    N2O–N = 18 kg. Mass of N2O = 18 × 44.02/28.02 = 28.3 kg. CO2e = 28.3 × 273 = 7720 kg ≈ 7.7 t CO2e.

    Marking: 1 mark for 18 kg; 1 mark for converting N to N2O; 1 mark for CO2e.

  4. (d)

    Part d: Suggest one way to reduce these emissions without reducing crop yield. 1 mark

    Worked solution for question B4 part d

    For example: split applications that match crop uptake; precision (variable-rate) spreading; nitrification inhibitors; legumes in the rotation to fix nitrogen naturally.

    Marking: 1 mark.

Question B5: How long will it last? 7 marks

Known reserves of a metal are 880 Mt, and current annual consumption is 26 Mt.

  1. (a)

    Part a: Calculate the static lifetime of the reserves. 1 mark

    Worked solution for question B5 part a

    880/26 = 34 years (33.8).

    Marking: 1 mark.

  2. (b)

    Part b: If consumption grows continuously at 3.0% per year, cumulative use after t years is (C0/r)(ert − 1). Calculate when the reserves run out. 4 marks

    Worked solution for question B5 part b

    880 = (26/0.030)(e0.030t − 1), so e0.030t = 1 + 0.030 × 880/26 = 2.015. Then t = ln(2.015)/0.030 = 23 years. Steady 3% growth cuts the lifetime by a third.

    Marking: 1 mark for setting up the equation; 1 mark for rearranging; 1 mark for the logarithm; 1 mark for the answer.

  3. (c)

    Part c: Give two reasons why the real lifetime could be longer than either estimate. 2 marks

    Worked solution for question B5 part c

    Any two of: higher prices make lower-grade deposits economic, so reserves grow; new discoveries; recycling; substitution by other materials; more efficient use.

    Marking: 1 mark each.
